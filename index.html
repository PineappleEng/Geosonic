Hearing Earth Open the app

Hear the Earth from orbit.

Hearing Earth turns photographs of our planet taken from space into slow, peaceful music. It sweeps across a picture from left to right, or through a video as it plays, so oceans, forests, deserts, clouds and the lights of cities each have a sound of their own. That gives people who are blind or have low vision a way into images that are usually only seen.

Listen to Earth Why sound?

Works with PNG and JPEG photos and WebM or MP4 videos, such as astronaut photos or time-lapses filmed from orbit. Export audio as MP3 or video as MP4. Everything runs in your browser; nothing is uploaded.

Seeing Earth from space, without seeing

Most of what people know about how Earth looks from orbit comes from pictures: the blue marble, hurricanes from above, the planet's night side lit up by cities. For blind and low-vision people, those pictures usually come down to one sentence of alt text. Sound can carry far more of the picture.

  • The layout becomes time

    The picture is played from left to right, so what you hear first is on the left and what you hear last is on the right. You can tell where the coastline is, where a storm starts and stops, and where day turns into night.

  • The same colours always sound the same

    Blue ocean always brings in the flowing arpeggio, green land the warm chords, and bright cloud and ice a lighter mode with sparkles. Black space is close to silence. After a few photos, listeners start to recognise what is below just from the sound.

  • Feeling, not just facts

    A caption says "Hurricane over the Atlantic". The sound adds how big the storm is, how bright and busy its clouds are, and the calm ocean around it. Hearing it alongside a spoken description gives a fuller picture than either one on its own.

  • Something to share

    Blind and sighted people can hear the same photo at the same time, in a classroom, a museum or at home, and talk about what they each notice. Turning space data into sound is a way to make science open to more people, not only those who can see a screen.

Sound works best alongside a spoken description, not as a replacement for one. At the moment, Space starts and pauses the music, but jumping to a spot in the picture needs a mouse or touch.

What Earth sounds like

A listening guide to the main things you'll find in a photo taken from orbit.

Oceans and lakes
Deep blue makes the arpeggio louder and more flowing, so open water sounds like gentle, rolling motion under the melody.
Forests and farmland
Green picks the chords, so wide areas of vegetation are heard as slow, warm changes in the harmony.
Deserts and red rock
Reds and ochres push the melody higher, so the Sahara or the Australian outback sings above the land around it.
Clouds, ice and snow
Bright white moves the music into a brighter mode and adds high sparkles; a polar ice cap or a thick bank of cloud sounds open and shining.
Storms, mountains and coastlines
Lots of fine detail makes the melody busier and the texture stronger, so the swirl of a hurricane or a jagged coast sounds more restless than calm sea.
Space and the night side
Darkness brings rests and a gentle minor mode. The black of space around the planet is close to silence, and city lights at night come through as scattered bright notes.

How it works

  1. Scan

    An image is cut into thin vertical slices, read from left to right; each slice is one beat. A video plays through time instead: each beat hears the frames shown during it, either through a strip that sweeps across the frame or across the whole frame at once (you choose), so the music lasts as long as the video and stays in sync with it.

  2. Measure

    Each slice is split into red, green and blue. From those come its brightness, hue, colourfulness, detail (how much the light varies inside the slice), where the light sits vertically and, for video, how much the picture moves.

  3. Compose

    Slices are grouped into bars and four-bar sections. Each section gets a key and a mode, chords change every two bars, and a melody is written that moves smoothly: small steps, long held notes, rests in the darkest places, and a harmony line a third below.

  4. Play

    An eight-part band plays the score in a vast, dark reverb, with echoes drifting between left and right. You choose the instrument for each part and can follow every note on the score as it plays.

What drives what

Red
The melody's pitch, and the harmony that follows it.
Green
Which chord plays: I, ii, IV, V or vi of the current key.
Blue
How loud the arpeggio is, and its rhythm in each bar.
Light
The mode, from bright Lydian down to gentle minor, and sparkles on the brightest slices.
Hue
The key of each section, moving gently around the circle of fifths, and the drone beneath it.
Detail and motion
How busy the melody is, and the strength of the texture.
Shape
The meter: 3/4 for portrait, 4/4 for landscape, 5/4 for panoramas.
Colourfulness
A little swing on the rare quicker notes; grey passages switch to a pentatonic melody.

The band

Eight parts, each with its own job. The first instrument listed is the default; you can swap any of them in the app while the music plays.
